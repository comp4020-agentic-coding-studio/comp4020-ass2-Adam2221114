Before the lab, have the lecture's five response types ready: apologise
now, acknowledge harm without a full apology, investigate first, act
first, delay. You'll be assigning one to each case, not picking whichever
feels most comfortable.

In the lab, you're given three deliberately ambiguous situations:

- A university department is told, anonymously, that its marking process
  may have disadvantaged some students, and has to respond to a public
  inquiry within a day — before anyone has checked whether the claim is
  accurate.
- Someone believes a friend forwarded a private message to a third party.
  The friend hasn't been asked yet.
- A driver has clearly, visibly hit a parked car. No question of fault.
  The car's owner isn't there.

For each, choose one of the five responses and defend it: what does this
choice get right that the other four get wrong, and what does it cost
you that a different choice wouldn't? "Investigate first" risks looking
evasive and leaves a harmed party waiting; "apologise now" risks getting
the facts wrong; "act first" risks presuming what the right action even
is before anyone's asked.

For the case you find most difficult, check your delay against the
lecture's test: is the time you're proposing going to produce new
information the response actually needs, or is the harmed party simply
waiting while you decide what to say?

If your pair is genuinely split down the middle on which response to
choose for a case, don't resolve it by picking whoever argues more
confidently. Write down both choices and both costs, and check whether
the disagreement is really about the facts of the case — how much time
is actually available, how bad the visible harm already looks — or about
how much weight each of you puts on looking responsive versus getting it
right; those are different disagreements and call for different next
steps.

Afterwards, compare your driver case with another group's. That one has
the least ambiguity about fault — see whether that changes which of the
five responses you land on, and why.
