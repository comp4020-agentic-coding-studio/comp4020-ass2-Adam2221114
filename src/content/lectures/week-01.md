Four messages, all of which someone has probably sent you:

> I'm sorry you feel that way.

> I was exhausted and wasn't thinking straight.

> I did it because you'd already ignored me twice.

> I shouldn't have said that, and I'm sorry it hurt you.

Only the last one is an apology. The first names a feeling and puts it on the
other person, not the speaker. The second explains a cause without owning the
act. The third argues the act was warranted. Only the fourth does both things
an apology actually requires: names the wrong as the speaker's, and accepts
responsibility for it rather than accounting for it.

## Regret, excuse, justification

- **Regret** describes an internal state ("I wish that hadn't happened") and
  commits to nothing.
- **Excuse** offers a cause for the act, which can lower blame without
  removing it.
- **Justification** argues the act was the right call given the
  circumstances, which denies there was anything to apologise for.
- **Apology** accepts that the act was wrong and that the speaker is
  answerable for it, regardless of cause or context.

The four are easy to blend in a single sentence, which is exactly how a
non-apology gets produced without anyone intending one: "I'm sorry, but you'd
been ignoring me" starts as an apology and ends as a justification, and the
justification is what actually governs how the sentence lands.

## Why the distinction is load-bearing

None of this is a vocabulary exercise. Confusing regret for apology is why "I
feel bad about it" gets offered as if it settled something. Confusing excuse
for apology is why "I was stressed" gets treated as a repair. The distinction
matters because only one of the four commits the speaker to anything.

Week 2 takes the fourth category apart: acknowledgment, responsibility,
regret, and remedy are not one move but four, and an apology can contain some
of them without containing all.
