Four messages, all of which someone has probably sent you:

> I'm sorry you feel that way.

> I was exhausted and wasn't thinking straight.

> I did it because you'd already ignored me twice.

> I shouldn't have said that, and I'm sorry it hurt you.

Only the last one is an apology. The first names a feeling and puts it on the
other person, not the speaker. The second explains a cause without owning the
act. The third argues the act was warranted. Only the fourth does both things
an apology actually requires: names the wrong as the speaker's, and accepts
responsibility for it rather than accounting for it.

## Regret, excuse, justification

- **Regret** describes an internal state ("I wish that hadn't happened") and
  commits to nothing.
- **Excuse** offers a cause for the act, which can lower blame without
  removing it.
- **Justification** argues the act was the right call given the
  circumstances, which denies there was anything to apologise for.
- **Apology** accepts that the act was wrong and that the speaker is
  answerable for it, regardless of cause or context.

The four are easy to blend in a single sentence, which is exactly how a
non-apology gets produced without anyone intending one: "I'm sorry, but you'd
been ignoring me" starts as an apology and ends as a justification, and the
justification is what actually governs how the sentence lands.

## Why the distinction is load-bearing

None of this is a vocabulary exercise. Confusing regret for apology is why "I
feel bad about it" gets offered as if it settled something. Confusing excuse
for apology is why "I was stressed" gets treated as a repair. The distinction
matters because only one of the four commits the speaker to anything.

## A message that resists sorting

Not every real message drops cleanly into one box. Take: "I'm sorry, I really
am, I just don't know what else I could have done." The first half is
apology-shaped — first person, present tense, the word itself. The second
half is a justification wearing an apology's opening clause, and it's doing
more work than the opening: it asks the listener to conclude no better action
existed, which is exactly the claim a justification makes. Sorted as a single
category, this message is a justification with an apology-shaped preamble,
not a genuine apology that happens to explain itself. The categories aren't
failing here — the message is mixing two moves in one sentence, and the
sorting exercise's job is to notice the seam, not to force a single label
onto something that is doing two things at once.

Week 2 takes the fourth category apart: acknowledgment, responsibility,
regret, and remedy are not one move but four, and an apology can contain some
of them without containing all.

## In class: was that even an apology?

Before class, find the message. It has to be real — something actually
sent or received, not a hypothetical. If it's one you're not comfortable
sharing verbatim, bring a close paraphrase; the wording matters less than
the shape of the move it makes.

In class, messages get swapped and sorted, blind, into the four
categories above: regret, excuse, justification, apology. The person who
brought the message doesn't get to sort their own — someone else does,
and has to say why out loud. Disagreements are the point: the same
sentence often reads as an excuse to one person and an apology to
another, and the argument about which is happening is more useful than
either verdict alone.

If your pair can't agree on which category a message belongs to after a
real attempt — not thirty seconds of shrugging — don't force a
resolution before moving on. Write down both readings and what each one
is keying off; the message that resists a clean verdict is usually more
useful to your one-sentence definition than one that sorted easily,
because it tells you what your definition has to be able to handle, not
just what it has to match.

Afterwards, each pair produces one sentence: "an apology is a statement
that ___." It has to survive being tested against every message the pair
looked at, including the ones that argued the hardest. That sentence is
what Week 2 starts from — and immediately complicates.
