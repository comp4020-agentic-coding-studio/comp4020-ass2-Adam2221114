Week 1 landed on a one-line test: an apology names the wrong and accepts
responsibility for it. That test is good enough to catch a justification
dressed as an apology, but it treats "apology" as one move. It isn't.

Take a real one, from a company statement after a data breach:

> We're sorry this happened. Our systems were compromised by a
> sophisticated attack. We are resetting all passwords and offering a
> year of credit monitoring. We take the trust our customers place in us
> extremely seriously.

Marked up, that statement contains:

- **Acknowledgment of harm** — "We're sorry this happened." Names that
  something bad occurred, to someone.
- **Acceptance of responsibility** — conspicuously absent. "Our systems
  were compromised" describes an event that happened to the company, not
  something the company did or failed to do.
- **Expression of regret** — folded into the same opening sentence as the
  acknowledgment, and doing no separate work of its own here.
- **Commitment to change or remedy** — present, concretely: password
  resets and credit monitoring are real actions with a real cost.

Three of four components are there. The one that's missing is
responsibility — and its absence is why the statement reads, correctly, as
damage control rather than an apology, no matter how expensive the remedy
is.

## Why four, and why they're separable

Each component answers a different question a wronged person actually
asks: *did you notice? do you accept it was on you? do you feel bad about
it? will it happen again?* A statement can answer three of those and dodge
the fourth, and readers reliably notice which one is missing even when they
can't name it. That's the diagnostic value of the split: it turns "that
didn't feel like a real apology" into "which component was missing," a
question with an answer.

## A component that's granted and then taken back

A harder case than a missing component: one that's present and then
quietly withdrawn by a later clause in the same statement. Take a
variant of the breach notice: "We take full responsibility for this
incident, though we want to be clear the vulnerability was disclosed to
us less than 24 hours before it was exploited." The first clause is a
real acceptance of responsibility. The second doesn't contradict it
outright — it's phrased as context — but its actual function is to
pre-argue a defence for the company before anyone has asked for one,
which is Week 1's justification move, smuggled in after the
responsibility clause has already done its job of sounding accountable.
Marking this up as "responsibility: present" would miss what the
sentence is actually doing; the components aren't just checked off
individually, they have to be checked against what the rest of the
statement does to them.

None of this ranks the four against each other yet — that's next week's
question, once the anatomy is on the table.
