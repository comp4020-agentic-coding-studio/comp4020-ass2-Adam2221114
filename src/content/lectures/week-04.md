Four statements, all commonly filed under "apology":

> I'm sorry if I offended anyone.

> Mistakes were made.

> I'm sorry you feel that way.

> I take full responsibility for what happened. I was raised very
> differently and didn't realise how it would come across.

None of them work, and not for the same reason. Week 3's ablation test —
remove a piece, see what breaks — applies to each individually rather than
to all four at once.

**"I'm sorry if I offended anyone."** The word "if" converts a fact into a
hypothesis. Acknowledgment of harm requires asserting that something
happened; this sentence asserts nothing, it only entertains a possibility.
Remove "if" and the sentence becomes a real acknowledgment. That single
word is the whole malfunction.

**"Mistakes were made."** The passive voice deletes the agent. Acceptance
of responsibility needs someone to attach to, and this sentence is built
so that no one is available to attach to. Ask "made by whom?" and the
sentence has no answer built into it — which is the point of choosing it.

**"I'm sorry you feel that way."** This one reroutes the object of the
sentence. It isn't "I did something," it's "you're having a reaction" —
the harm has been relocated from the speaker's act to the listener's
feelings. Acknowledgment is present in form (the word "sorry" is right
there) and absent in function (nothing about the speaker's conduct is
named).

**"I take full responsibility... I was raised differently..."** This is
the hardest case, because the first sentence is a real, functioning
acceptance of responsibility — remove it and the passage stops working
entirely. The second sentence is the problem: it's an excuse, in exactly
Week 1's sense, and it quietly withdraws what the first sentence granted.
Test it by removing the second sentence instead: the apology survives
intact, which means the second sentence was never doing repair work. It
was only softening the first.

## Same symptom, different mechanism

All four produce the same complaint — "that didn't feel like a real
apology" — but the ablation test locates a different point of failure in
each: a conditional, a missing agent, a redirected object, a withdrawn
concession. Treating "sorry if," "mistakes were made" and "sorry you feel
that way" as interchangeable entries on a list of bad phrases would be
accurate about the symptom and wrong about the cause, which is exactly
Week 3's point applied to failure instead of success: what's broken
depends on the case.

None of this asks whether any of these four people meant to produce a
non-apology, or drifted into one out of habit. Whether that distinction
matters is Week 5's question.
