Start with a limit: nothing here claims to know what anyone privately
felt when they apologised. "They didn't really mean it" isn't a claim
this course can check. What can be checked is what an apology's wording,
timing, and cost accomplish — regardless of what was going on
internally when it was written.

Two cases.

A public figure issues a carefully worded, apparently contrite statement
the day before a scheduled interview that would otherwise have opened
with the same topic. Whether the regret is genuine is not observable.
What is observable: the timing closes out a news cycle on the speaker's
terms rather than the interviewer's. That's a real effect of the
statement, sincere or not.

A colleague, asked what happened to a shared project, gives their
manager a specific, self-incriminating account when a vaguer one would
have protected them just as well with the person they'd let down. The
specific version costs them something with their manager that the vague
version wouldn't have. That cost is evidence of what the apology was
built to prioritise, independent of what the colleague felt while giving
it.

## Four things an apology can do for the speaker

- **Relational repair** — restore the relationship on the harmed party's
  terms.
- **Self-protection** — limit damage to the speaker's standing.
- **Reputation management** — control how the speaker is perceived by
  people who weren't harmed.
- **Closure-seeking** — end the discomfort of the situation, for the
  speaker, regardless of whether the harmed party is satisfied.

These aren't mutually exclusive, and none of them is the same axis as
"was this a real apology" from Weeks 1–4. An apology can be
component-complete in Week 2's sense and still be primarily doing
reputation management. Having all four components doesn't guarantee
repair is what's happening.

## A test that doesn't require telepathy

Instead of asking what someone meant, ask: what would have to be true of
the wording if this function were the primary one? Reputation management
predicts wording vague enough to survive many audiences, timed to news
cycles. Self-protection predicts minimal concrete admissions. Closure-
seeking predicts language asking for the matter to be considered settled.
Repair predicts wording answerable to what the harmed party specifically
needed, which usually means it can't be drafted without knowing what they
needed. Check the actual statement against the prediction; that's
evidence, not a guess about interiority.

Week 4's "sorry you feel that way" fails as repair — nothing is
acknowledged as the speaker's doing. It can still succeed at
closure-seeking: the sentence ends the exchange, for the speaker, whether
or not the other person is satisfied. Failing on one function doesn't
mean failing on all of them.

All of this has assumed one listener. Once there's an audience — people
who weren't harmed but are watching — a single sentence can pursue
different functions for different addressees at once. That's Week 6.
