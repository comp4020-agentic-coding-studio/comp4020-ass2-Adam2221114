Same incident, two apologies.

Sent privately, to the person it happened to:

> I shouldn't have brought that up in front of everyone. That was out of
> line, I'm sorry, and I get why you're upset.

Released publicly, about the same incident:

> I want to address recent comments of mine that some found hurtful. I
> take that feedback seriously and am committed to doing better.

The private version names the act plainly, because the recipient already
knows what happened and needs it acknowledged, not described. The public
version is vaguer on every count: "comments... that some found hurtful"
describes an effect without naming the act, in a construction close
enough to Week 4's passive deflection that it's worth asking whether it's
the same failure. It usually isn't. Full detail in the public statement
would mean re-describing, to strangers, exactly what was said to the
person it was said to — which re-exposes them, not the speaker. The
vagueness here can be doing something other than evasion: protecting the
harmed party's privacy at the cost of the observers' need for specifics.

## Two addressees, one sentence

A public apology has at least two audiences at once: the harmed party,
who needs the act named and answered to; and observers, who need enough
information to judge whether accountability actually occurred, plus
reassurance that the behaviour has consequences. These pull in different
directions. Full specificity serves observers' need to judge and can
violate the harmed party's need not to be re-exposed. Vagueness protects
the harmed party and reads to observers as evasive — which is exactly why
Week 4's passive-voice diagnosis doesn't automatically transfer here: the
same surface move can be deflection in one case and privacy protection in
another, and telling them apart requires asking who the vagueness is
actually protecting.

Week 5's four functions don't disappear once there's an audience; they
split across addressees. Reputation management targets observers.
Repair targets the harmed party. "I take that feedback seriously" is
built to register as an accountability signal for observers while
carrying almost no specific content for the one person who already knows
what was said and needed something else entirely — one sentence, two
jobs, aimed at two different listeners.

This doesn't resolve the tension between the two audiences, only names
it. Week 7 asks whether timing and medium can shift which audience's
needs end up mattering more.
