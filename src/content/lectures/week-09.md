A friend who has repeatedly cancelled plans gives a component-complete
apology: names what they did, accepts it was on them, says they're
genuinely sorry, and describes what they'll do differently. The person
they've let down says: "I hear you, thanks for saying that" — and over
the following months, keeps a little more distance, declines a couple of
invitations, doesn't fully return to how things were.

What happened here is not one thing, and treating it as one thing is
where most confusion about apologies comes from. At minimum, seven
different outcomes get run together under "the apology worked" or "the
apology didn't work":

- **Hearing** — the apology was received and understood as an apology.
- **Acknowledgment** — the recipient confirms it happened, without
  saying anything about whether it was good enough.
- **Acceptance** — the recipient judges the apology itself adequate on
  its own terms.
- **Forgiveness** — the recipient releases the standing the act had
  against the person, internally, whether or not they say so.
- **Reconciliation** — the relationship resumes its previous shape.
- **Repair** — something concretely damaged gets restored: trust
  demonstrated over time, a habit actually changed, a debt paid.
- **Closure** — the matter feels finished, for whoever it feels finished
  for, independent of any of the above.

"I hear you, thanks for saying that" is hearing and acknowledgment,
clearly. It might also be acceptance — the apology can be judged good as
an apology — without being forgiveness, reconciliation, or repair, all of
which take time the sentence doesn't promise. The distance that follows
isn't evidence the apology failed. It's evidence that acceptance doesn't
carry the other four with it automatically. Asking "was the apology good"
and asking "was I forgiven" are different questions, and this course has
only ever been equipped to study the first one — Week 5's caution about
reading private motive from wording applies just as much to reading a
recipient's inner state from their silence.

## Where recipient authority does have limits

None of this makes the recipient's response beyond examination. Consider
a different case: someone says the apology wasn't specific enough; it
gets revised to be specific; they say it came too late; the timing was
already about as fast as Week 7 would call reasonable; they say it wasn't
delivered face to face; it's redelivered that way. At some point, a
pattern like this is itself a fact worth naming: a demand that shifts to
stay unsatisfied by whatever is offered is different from a standard the
apology hasn't met yet. Naming that pattern isn't calling the recipient
wrong to feel however they feel — it's noticing that "how good was this
apology" and "will this particular person ever call it enough" are two
different questions, and only one of them is answerable by looking at the
apology.

A public case makes the same point from the other side: someone
harmed says, in front of cameras, "I forgive them," under visible
social pressure to be seen as gracious. Whether that matches anything
felt privately can't be verified — but what the statement accomplishes
publicly can be observed regardless: it ends public scrutiny of the
harmed party's response and casts them as magnanimous, whether or not
forgiveness, in the private sense, occurred at all.

Week 8 asked who has to apologise. Today asked who decides whether it
counted. Neither question has a mechanical answer, and conflating them —
treating "they didn't forgive me" as proof the apology failed, or "they
said the words" as proof it succeeded — is the mistake this week exists
to prevent.
