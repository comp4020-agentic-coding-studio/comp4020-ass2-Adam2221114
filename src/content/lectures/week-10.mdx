A courier company loses a customer's package. Support responds well by
every standard this course has built: they name exactly what happened, no
"if," no passive deflection, a clear "this is on us," and a refund
processed the same day. By Weeks 1 through 9, this apology has almost
nothing wrong with it.

Three months later, the same customer's package is damaged at the same
depot, by the same handling process, in the same way. The refund was
real. Nothing else was.

This isn't a story about insincerity — the apology can be entirely
sincere and still change nothing, because an apology and a repair are two
different kinds of act, and being good at one says nothing about whether
the other happened.

## Six things that are not the same thing

- **Apology as an utterance** — the speech act itself, everything Weeks
  1–9 studied: components, function, audience, timing.
- **Acknowledgment** — an external admission that harm occurred, which
  can exist with or without a well-formed apology attached to it.
- **Restitution** — compensation for what was actually lost: the refund,
  the replaced item, the corrected record.
- **Behaviour change** — the person or organisation responsible
  personally does something differently going forward.
- **Structural repair** — the conditions that produced the harm are
  altered: a process changed, a policy rewritten, a safeguard added.
- **Prevention of recurrence** — the actual outcome all of the above are
  supposed to add up to, and the only one of the six that a second
  incident can directly falsify.

The courier case had a good utterance and real restitution. It had no
structural repair, because nothing about how packages were handled at
that depot was ever examined. Prevention of recurrence therefore never
had a mechanism behind it — the promise "we'll make sure this doesn't
happen again" was, in the most literal sense, unsupported by anything the
company actually did.

## Behaviour change is not the same as prevention, either

A slightly different case makes the same point at a smaller scale: a
partner who repeatedly misses commitments delivers a genuinely excellent
apology every time — specific, responsible, no hedging — and each time
intends, sincerely, to do better. The recurrence isn't evidence the
apologies were fake. It's evidence that intending to change and actually
changing are different events, and that a wording this good, repeated
enough times without the second event ever showing up, starts to function
as something other than what it was built to be.

## When restitution is impossible, not just skipped

The courier and the partner both had a repair available and didn't
complete it. A harder case has no available repair at all: a driver
who caused a permanent injury can apologise well, and there is no
structural change or restitution that returns the other person's body to
how it was. Prevention of recurrence — fixing the road, the training, the
policy — is still possible and still owed, but it repairs the next
person's risk, not this person's loss. Calling that "repair" for the
person who was actually harmed would overstate what happened; the honest
description is that some harms only ever get a subset of the six items on
this list, permanently, and an apology's job in that case is to be
accurate about which subset is still available rather than to imply the
rest is coming.

Week 9 already separated forgiveness, acceptance, and reconciliation from
each other. Repair belongs on that same list, not folded into any of
them — a recipient can forgive, accept, and even reconcile, and the harm
can still be sitting there completely unrepaired. This isn't a lesson
about actions mattering more than words. It's that words and actions are
answering different questions, and only one of them is answered by an
apology, however well built.
