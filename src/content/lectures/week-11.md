A complaint surfaces on a team. Within the hour, the team lead posts:
"I'm sorry if anyone was affected by my decision." At this point, no one
— including the team lead — has established what decision caused the
problem, or whether it was the team lead's call at all.

The conditional "if" here isn't Week 4's evasion. It's what happens when
an apology is issued before there's anything specific yet to
acknowledge — the vagueness is a symptom of timing, not of dishonesty.
And it carries two real costs. First, it can settle the question of who
is responsible before that question has actually been investigated,
foreclosing the version of events that would let anyone say anything
specific. Second, if the facts turn out different — someone else made
the call, the harm was of a different kind entirely — the apology has to
be revised or withdrawn, which reads worse than saying nothing yet would
have.

A costlier version of the same mistake: a company, after a safety
incident, issues a full public apology and a remedy commitment the same
day, before any investigation. The investigation later finds a different
root cause. The remedy they promised turns out to be aimed at the wrong
problem, and the company now has to explain, publicly, why its own fix
doesn't fix anything — a worse position than having said, initially,
"something went wrong, we're finding out what."

## This isn't an argument for silence

A different case shows the opposite failure. A person clearly at fault
already knows they're at fault, and delays anyway — behind language like
"we're still looking into it" — not to learn anything, but to manage
their own legal or reputational exposure while the harmed party is left
without any acknowledgment at all. That wait is its own harm: being kept
in suspense reads as being ignored.

The test that separates this from Week 7's legitimate delay is what the
time is actually being spent on. Week 7 defended delay that produces
real understanding the apology needs. This delay produces nothing except
distance between the incident and any response to it — the facts are
already known, and the wait serves only the person who caused the harm.

Between "apologise immediately" and "say nothing," there's a third move
this week is actually arguing for: acknowledgment without a full
apology. "Something went wrong. I don't yet know the details, but I take
it seriously and I'll find out" commits to nothing false, closes nothing
prematurely, and still tells the harmed party they haven't been ignored.

Every week before this one treated the apology as something that should
already exist, waiting to be built, fixed, or better delivered. This week
is the one place that assumption gets challenged directly: sometimes the
task isn't producing a better apology. It's recognising that one
shouldn't be produced yet.
