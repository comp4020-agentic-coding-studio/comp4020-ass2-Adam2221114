Take one sentence: "I'm sorry for what happened. I should have handled
this better, and I'll make sure it doesn't happen again." Run it through
everything this course has built, one variable at a time.

**Component check (Weeks 2–3).** Acknowledgment is here, but thin —
"what happened" names nothing. Responsibility is present but softened by
"handled" rather than a direct claim of fault. No explicit regret beyond
"sorry." A remedy commitment closes it. Ablate the remedy clause: still
reads as an apology, weaker. Ablate "I should have handled this better":
what's left barely claims anything, and stops functioning as an apology
at all — in this sentence, responsibility is the load-bearing part,
though Week 3 already warned that won't hold for every sentence.

**Non-apology check (Week 4).** No "if," no passive deletion of the
agent, no redirection onto the listener's feelings. It survives the
surface test. Its vagueness isn't yet Week 4's failure mode — it's just
underspecified, which is a separate problem.

**Function (Week 5).** Whether this serves repair, reputation, or
closure-seeking can't be settled from the sentence. The words are
identical in all three cases.

**Audience (Week 6).** Said privately to someone who already knows the
details, the vagueness may be entirely acceptable — they don't need
"what happened" spelled out. Said publicly to people without that
context, the same vagueness reads as evasive.

**Timing and medium (Week 7).** Sent by text within minutes: reads
rushed, possibly closure-seeking for the speaker. Delivered face to face
after time spent establishing what actually happened, with the vague
parts now filled in: reads as considered.

**Power (Week 8).** Said by someone whose position lets them decide
unilaterally whether "handling this better" ever actually happens, the
remedy clause costs the speaker nothing to say and nothing to break.

**Acceptance (Week 9).** A recipient's "okay" tells you the sentence was
heard, possibly accepted. It tells you nothing about forgiveness,
reconciliation, or repair, which are separate events this sentence
cannot produce by itself.

**Repair and premature timing (Weeks 10–11).** "I'll make sure it
doesn't happen again" is a claim about the future the sentence cannot
verify — its success as repair depends entirely on what happens after
it's said, not on how it's worded. And whether the moment was even right
for it depends on whether the facts behind "what happened" were actually
known yet.

## What "perfect" can mean, twelve weeks in

One fixed sentence produced eight different verdicts, because the
sentence was never the whole object of study — the apology only exists
in combination with who said it, to whom, when, through what channel,
from what position, followed by what. "Perfect" cannot mean one wording
that clears every version of that combination, because no wording
survives all eight variables unchanged.

The course's own title asked the question first. The most honest answer
this semester can give is: a good apology is a situated design decision
whose success depends on what harm occurred, who is involved, what
function the apology needs to perform, and what happens after the
words. That is a description of what to check, not a formula — the
right weighting of harm, audience, power, timing, and aftermath is
exactly what changes from case to case, and pretending otherwise would
undo eleven weeks of work in a single closing slogan. More components,
more solemnity, more remedy isn't "more perfect" if it's the wrong size
for what actually happened — an elaborate public apology for a private,
minor matter is mismatched, not superior.
