Before the session, find the message. It has to be real — something
actually sent or received, not a hypothetical. If it's one you're not
comfortable sharing verbatim, bring a close paraphrase; the wording matters
less than the shape of the move it makes.

In the session, messages get swapped and sorted, blind, into the lecture's
four categories: regret, excuse, justification, apology. The person who
brought the message doesn't get to sort their own — someone else does, and
has to say why out loud. Disagreements are the point: the same sentence
often reads as an excuse to one person and an apology to another, and the
argument about which is happening is more useful than either verdict alone.

Afterwards, each pair produces one sentence: "an apology is a statement
that ___." It has to survive being tested against every message the pair
looked at, including the ones that argued the hardest. That sentence is
what Week 2 starts from — and immediately complicates.
