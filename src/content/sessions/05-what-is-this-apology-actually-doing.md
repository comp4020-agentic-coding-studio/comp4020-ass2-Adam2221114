Before the session, find two apologies responding to broadly comparable
incidents — two public figures apologising for similar conduct, two
companies apologising for similar failures, or two people you know
apologising for a similar kind of mistake. They don't need to be
identical situations, just close enough to compare.

In the session, for each apology, work out which of the lecture's four
functions — repair, self-protection, reputation management, closure —
its actual wording, timing, and cost seem built to serve. Evidence only:
point at a specific phrase, a publication date relative to other events,
or a concession that cost the speaker something. "I think they were being
fake" is not an answer the session accepts; "this was released the same
day as an unrelated announcement" is.

Then, for one of your two apologies, write down what its wording would
have to contain for repair to be the primary function — something
answerable to what the specific harmed party needed, which usually
requires knowing what they needed. Check whether that's actually there.
If it isn't, say what function the apology serves instead, and what in
the text tells you so.

Afterwards, compare your two apologies directly: did the more
repair-oriented one cost the speaker something the other didn't?
