Before the session, find a real public apology statement — a company
statement, a public figure's post, a press release — long enough to have
more than two or three sentences.

In the session, go through it line by line. For each line, decide who it
is actually built to serve: the harmed party specifically, observers who
weren't harmed, or genuinely both. Point at what makes the call — a
vague phrase that protects the harmed party's privacy at the cost of
observers' need to judge is a different diagnosis from a vague phrase
that protects no one but the speaker. Mark at least one line that clearly
serves observers at the harmed party's expense, or the reverse.

Then rewrite the statement to prioritise the harmed party over observers:
name the act specifically, address what they in particular need
acknowledged, and stop optimising for how the statement reads to
strangers. When you're done, say explicitly what the rewrite gives up —
what an observer would no longer be able to judge or be reassured about,
compared to the original.

Afterwards, trade rewrites with another pair. Check each other's cost
list: did the rewrite actually sacrifice public accountability, or does
it turn out the original wasn't serving observers as well as it first
appeared either?
