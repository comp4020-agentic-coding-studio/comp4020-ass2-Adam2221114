Before the session, construct (or bring) two versions of one structurally
similar incident: the same kind of mistake, made once by someone with
less power toward someone with more, and once by someone with more power
toward someone with less. Keep the mistake itself as close to identical
as you can; only the direction of the power relationship should change.

In the session, for each pair, argue one of two positions and be ready to
defend it: either the obligation to apologise genuinely changes depending
on who holds power, or the obligation stays the same and only the
likelihood, speed, or form of the apology changes because of what the
more powerful person can get away with. Use Week 7's variables as
evidence, not as the whole argument: does a personal response happen at
all, how quickly, and through what channel. A senior person who never
personally responds hasn't necessarily lost the obligation to apologise —
they may simply have avoided it at low cost to themselves.

Trade your pair with another group and see whether they land on the same
side of the argument using your incidents. Where you disagree, identify
exactly which claim you disagree about — the obligation, or only what
actually happens in practice.

Afterwards, note one case, real or constructed, where a powerful person
did apologise promptly and personally — and be ready to say why that
case doesn't disprove the pattern, if it doesn't.
