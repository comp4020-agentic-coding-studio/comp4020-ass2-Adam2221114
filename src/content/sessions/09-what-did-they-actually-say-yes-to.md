Before the session, have the lecture's six terms ready to hand —
hearing, acknowledgment, acceptance, forgiveness, reconciliation, repair,
closure — you'll be sorting responses against them, not against a vague
sense of "worked" or "didn't work."

In the session, you'll be given one well-constructed apology and a set of
different recipient responses to it: a flat "fine, apology accepted"
followed by no change in contact; "I forgive you, but I don't think I can
trust you the same way"; someone who says nothing at all but quietly
resumes normal contact; a public "I forgive them" delivered in front of
an audience; and at least one recipient who keeps finding a new reason
the apology falls short no matter how it's revised. Classify each
response against the lecture's terms — more than one term can apply to
the same response, and that's expected, not a sign you've done it wrong.

Where your classification differs from a partner's, defend it: point at
what in the response licenses acceptance rather than mere acknowledgment,
or forgiveness rather than reconciliation. For the response that keeps
shifting its objection, decide whether it's a legitimate standard not yet
met or a demand no apology could satisfy — and if it's the latter, say
what you think the recipient is actually asking for, since it isn't a
better apology.

Afterwards, note which of the six terms your group disagreed about most.
That's usually the distinction people flatten in ordinary conversation,
and the one worth being precise about.
