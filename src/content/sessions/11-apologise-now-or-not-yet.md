Before the session, have the lecture's five response types ready:
apologise now, acknowledge harm without a full apology, investigate
first, act first, delay. You'll be assigning one to each case, not
picking whichever feels most comfortable.

In the session, you're given three deliberately ambiguous situations:

- A university department is told, anonymously, that its marking
  process may have disadvantaged some students, and has to respond to a
  public inquiry within a day — before anyone has checked whether the
  claim is accurate.
- Someone believes a friend forwarded a private message to a third
  party. The friend hasn't been asked yet.
- A driver has clearly, visibly hit a parked car. No question of fault.
  The car's owner isn't there.

For each, choose one of the five responses and defend it: what does this
choice get right that the other four get wrong, and what does it cost
you that a different choice wouldn't? "Investigate first" risks looking
evasive and leaves a harmed party waiting; "apologise now" risks getting
the facts wrong; "act first" risks presuming what the right action even
is before anyone's asked.

For the case you find most difficult, check your delay against the
lecture's test: is the time you're proposing going to produce new
information the response actually needs, or is the harmed party simply
waiting while you decide what to say?

Afterwards, compare your driver case with another group's. That one has
the least ambiguity about fault — see whether that changes which of the
five responses you land on, and why.
