Before the session, find your Week 1 message again, and the one-sentence
definition your pair wrote and put your name to. Bring both exactly as
they were — this only works if you don't quietly improve them first.

In the session, analyse that same message from the start, using
everything since: which components it has and which are doing the work
in this specific case (Weeks 2–3), whether it survives the non-apology
test (Week 4), what function it most plausibly serves (Week 5), how its
audience, timing, and medium shape what it means (Weeks 6–7), what power
relationship it sits inside (Week 8), what accepting it would and
wouldn't settle (Week 9), and what repair — if any — it would actually
require, and whether it was even the right moment for it (Weeks 10–11).

Then write the comparison directly: quote your Week 1 sentence, and say,
specifically, what it assumed that turned out not to hold, or what it
couldn't have accounted for because Week 1 hadn't built the vocabulary
yet. This isn't about whether your Week 1 pair was careless — the point
is to make visible exactly how much the same message can be found to be
doing once the full framework is applied to it.

Afterwards, read your comparison next to another pair's. Notice whether
the thing your Week 1 definition missed is the same kind of thing theirs
missed, or something different — that difference is, itself, evidence of
which components of "what makes an apology work" are easiest to overlook
starting out.
