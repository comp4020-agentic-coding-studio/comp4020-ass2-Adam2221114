Before the session, pick an apology you haven't looked at through the
four-component lens yet. It can be personal or public, written or
something you remember well enough to transcribe. Don't pre-sort it — the
marking-up happens in the session itself.

In the session, pairs annotate their partner's apology using the four
components from the lecture: acknowledgment, responsibility, regret,
remedy. Underline or bracket the exact phrase doing each job. Some
components will have no phrase at all — mark that as clearly as a
component that's present. Then, for every component that is present,
answer one extra question: is it doing real work, or is it there because
an apology is expected to have one? A "commitment to change" that's just
"this won't happen again" with no mechanism behind it is present and
empty at the same time.

If a partner's apology turns out to have every component clearly
present, with nothing to flag as hollow, don't manufacture a doubt just
to have something to report. Say instead which component came closest
to feeling redundant with another — regret and acknowledgment tend to
blur together in real apologies — and what specifically would have to
change in the wording to actually collapse them into one.

Afterwards, each pair reports one component from their apology that
technically appears but earns its place the least. Keep that example —
Week 3 asks which of the four actually carries the weight, and a
component that's present but hollow is exactly the case that argument
needs.
