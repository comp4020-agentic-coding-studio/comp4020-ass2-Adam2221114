# What Is an Apology, Actually?

What distinguishes an apology from something that merely sounds like one?

---

## Six messages

Read them cold. Don't sort them yet.

> That's awful — I'm so sorry that happened to you.

> Yeah, I know. I was there.

> I really wish I hadn't said that.

> I was running on about four hours' sleep. I wasn't thinking straight.

> You'd already cancelled on me twice, so I figured it didn't matter.

> I was short with you this morning, and there's no excuse for it. I'm
> sorry.

---

## What is each one actually doing?

- Which one only reports how the speaker feels?
- Which one only confirms that something happened?
- Which one explains a cause, without owning the act?
- Which one argues the act was warranted?
- Which one names the act as the speaker's own — and answers for it?

Only one does that last thing. The other five are close enough to be
mistaken for it, which is the entire problem this week is about.

---

## Apology vs. regret

Regret describes an internal state. It commits the speaker to nothing
beyond the sentence.

> I wish I hadn't yelled.

That's true whether or not the speaker thinks yelling was wrong, or plans
to do it again. Regret doesn't require either.

---

## Apology vs. excuse

An excuse supplies a cause. A cause can lower blame — it doesn't remove
it.

> I was exhausted. That's why I snapped at you.

The exhaustion might be entirely true. It still isn't an admission that
snapping was wrong — just an account of why it happened.

---

## Apology vs. justification

A justification argues the act was the right call. If it was the right
call, there's nothing to apologise for — that's the actual claim, even
inside a sentence that opens with "sorry."

> Sorry, but you'd cancelled on me twice.

The "sorry" is doing no work here. The "but" is the whole sentence.

---

## Apology vs. acknowledgment

Acknowledgment confirms that something happened. It doesn't say who
answers for it.

> Yeah, I know. I was there.

That can be completely honest and still commit to nothing. Acknowledgment
is often the first move inside an apology. On its own, it isn't the
apology.

---

## Apology vs. sympathy

Sympathy expresses concern about someone's situation, not responsibility
for causing it.

> That's awful — I'm so sorry that happened to you.

Said by a bystander, that's an ordinary kindness. Said by the person who
caused it, the same words describe the harm from a safe distance instead
of claiming it.

---

## What actually changes

All five of the statements above can be sincere and still avoid one
specific move: naming the act as the speaker's own and accepting an
answer for it.

Responsibility isn't a tone. It's a claim — *this was mine, and I'm
answerable for it* — not "I feel bad," not "here's why," not "you'd have
done the same."

Nothing on the last five slides makes that claim. That's the actual
line, not whether the word "sorry" shows up.

---

{/* _class: quote */}

> An apology names a wrong as the speaker's own, and accepts
> responsibility for it — regardless of what caused it, or how it feels
> to say.

A provisional definition. Built to be broken.

---

## A case that doesn't sit still

> I'm sorry — I know I hurt you, but I still think I was right to say it.

The first half names the wrong and accepts it. The second half reopens
exactly the justification Week 1's definition was supposed to rule out.
Does one sentence get to make both moves? Hold that question. Don't
answer it yet.

---

## This definition will not survive intact

It's provisional on purpose. Next week takes "apology" apart into
separate components, and several weeks after that put real pressure on
what "responsibility" even means here. Treat today's definition as a
starting position worth defending — not a formula worth memorising.
